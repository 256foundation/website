**The 256 Foundation applauds the arrival of the first retail product developed with Mujina, the open-source Bitcoin mining firmware: the [RY3T Nova](https://nova.ry3t.com/en/), in development at [RY3T](https://ry3t.com/en/) of Wil, Switzerland.**

We had nothing to do with it. That is precisely why we are announcing it.

The Nova is a solar self-consumption appliance. When a home's battery is full and the array is still producing, the Nova absorbs the surplus and turns it into Bitcoin — tracking the generation curve in seconds, ramping its power draw up and down to match what the panels are actually making, talking directly to the inverter or energy management system that runs the building. It does this by running **[Mujina](https://mujina.org)** on stock mining control boards. No aftermarket controller, no intermediary hardware, no closed-firmware ceiling.

To our knowledge, no other commercial mining product can follow a solar curve at this resolution. The reason is not a hardware advantage. It is that Mujina is open source, and its maintainers were free to treat power as a live control input rather than a configuration setting.

Mujina is a community project, owned by no one. The 256 Foundation kicked it off, stewards the repository, and funds the Core Contributors who maintain it — but it is GPLv3, and it is there for the taking. RY3T took it. They did not ask us, negotiate a license, or sign a partnership. They read the code, modified it for their system, and built a product around it, which is exactly what the license invites anyone to do.

That is the milestone worth marking. Not that the Foundation shipped something — we didn't, and it isn't our job to. It's that open-source mining firmware has reached a machine you will be able to buy.

## Why Anyone Wants a Miner That Follows the Sun

The appeal is not novelty. It is arithmetic.

Anyone with a solar array and a full battery faces the same question every sunny afternoon: what happens to the surplus? In most markets the answer is that it goes to the grid at the export or net-metering rate — and across much of Europe and the United States, that rate has been falling for years. Feed-in tariffs that once approached the retail price of electricity have been cut repeatedly. In a growing number of places, surplus midday solar earns a couple of cents per kilowatt-hour, nothing at all, or gets curtailed outright because the grid does not want it.

Bitcoin mining is a buyer of last resort for that electricity. A miner will consume surplus generation at whatever value the hashrate produces — and for a great many owners, in a great many hours of the year, that is worth more than the export rate on offer. The exact comparison depends on the local tariff, the machine's efficiency, the Bitcoin price, and network difficulty, so it is not universal and it is not static. But the structure of the trade is durable: exporting is a price you are given, and mining is a price you can find.

Every other flexible load in the house is already treated this way. Batteries, heat pumps, EV chargers, and hot water tanks all get scheduled against the solar curve by the same energy management systems. A miner is the one load that could absorb surplus at any hour, in any quantity, without needing somewhere to put the energy afterward — and it was the one load nobody could dispatch.

That is the product people have wanted for a decade. Nobody could build it, because the firmware would not let them.

## The Bottleneck Was Never the Hardware

ASICs have always been able to change power. The chips take a frequency and a voltage; dial them down and the machine draws less. The physics were never the problem.

The problem is that every firmware option on the market — the OEM firmware from the manufacturers and the aftermarket alternatives alike — was written for one deployment: a data center, running flat out, at a fixed setpoint, forever. In that world, changing power is a maintenance event. Nobody optimized for it, because nobody needed it. Closed firmware treats power as a configuration setting rather than a live control input.

- **Whatsminer stock firmware** takes roughly 40 minutes to respond to a wattage change, and reboots the mining process to do it.
- **Braiins** likewise restarts the mining process on a change, with a similar delay before the machine is productive again.
- **Auradine** built something genuinely more responsive — and then the company pivoted to AI. That firmware is no longer a path forward.

Forty minutes is not a control loop. A cloud front moves across an array in seconds. By the time a stock miner has finished rebooting to accept a new setpoint, the condition it was responding to is gone.

Companies worked around it anyway. Home Assistant integrations, external controllers, aftermarket relay hardware, and management layers bolted on top of miners that were never designed to be modulated — a whole industry of scaffolding built to compensate for a firmware limitation. The scaffolding worked, more or less. It also meant extra hardware, extra software, and an upper bound on responsiveness that no amount of integration work could lift.

You cannot fix a closed firmware from outside it. You have to replace it.

## Mujina: Power as a Live Input

[Mujina](https://mujina.org) is open-source Bitcoin mining firmware — GPLv3, written in Rust, running on Linux, and maintained by **Ryan Kuester**, a Core Contributor funded by the 256 Foundation. The [source is public](https://github.com/256foundation/mujina).

The relevant capability is unglamorous and decisive: **Mujina accepts a power target and settles on it in seconds, without rebooting the mining process.**

That single property is what turns a miner into a dispatchable load. Set 1,400W, and the machine is at 1,400W before the next cloud arrives. Set 2,900W, same. No restart, no dropped connection to the pool, no forty-minute penalty for changing your mind. The miner becomes something an inverter or an energy management system can actually steer.

Because Mujina is open source, that behavior is not a feature request filed with a manufacturer. It is code, in public, that anyone can read, run, and build on.

## What Was Demonstrated

The capability stack behind the Nova has been demonstrated publicly over recent months by **Schnitzel**, the engineer responsible for the Mujina integration work. Each of the following is documented in public, on real hardware, running Mujina.

### One control board, two chip generations

The world's first mixed-generation Bitcoin miner: three hashboards spanning two chip generations — S19j and S19k — on a single control board, 280 chips and roughly 110 TH/s, with each board hashing at its own frequency, simultaneously.

<!-- ASSET NEEDED: Schnitzel's mixed-hashboard tweet — link + screenshot -->

This is the kind of thing closed firmware structurally cannot do, because it dismantles the upgrade cycle. If a miner can mix generations, the consequences follow immediately:

- Upgrade one hashboard at a time instead of replacing an entire machine.
- Run the efficient boards continuously and fire up the older, cheaper boards only on surplus power.
- One machine with several break-even prices instead of one.
- "Obsolete" hashboards become the cheapest hashrate on Earth the moment they are run on energy that would otherwise be wasted.

Nobody selling new miners has an incentive to ship that firmware. That is precisely why it needed to be open source.

### Stepless power tracking

A single Nova following a power target from **1,000W to 3,000W — stepless, within seconds.** Point it at your inverter or your smart home controller and surplus solar becomes sats instead of a few cents of export credit.

<!-- ASSET NEEDED: Schnitzel's Nova power-tracking demo video — link + still/GIF -->

### Clustering

Three miners — with different hashboards and different control boards — following a single watt setting together, within seconds. Two distribution strategies:

- **Balance mode** spreads the target across all miners so each runs under-clocked, maximizing efficiency.
- **Largest-first mode** fills one miner completely before starting the next, minimizing the number of power transitions.

The control system runs on one of the miners itself. No separate Raspberry Pi, no external controller, no additional box in the rack.

## The Nova

RY3T, based in Wil, Switzerland, packages this into a product.

The **Nova** is their clustered model: up to four miners presented and controlled as a **single appliance**, scaling to **9kW**. You give it one power target; it distributes the load across its miners and reports back combined and per-miner hashrate, power, temperatures, fan state, pool connectivity, uptime, and error status.

Integration is over **Modbus TCP** — the protocol that building automation systems, PLCs, PV controllers, and energy management systems already speak. RY3T publishes the [full integration documentation and register map on GitHub](https://github.com/RY3T/ry3t-nova-integration-docs), with MQTT and an HTTP REST API listed as planned. That matters for the same reason the firmware does: the Nova is designed to be commanded by whatever already runs the building, rather than requiring its own parallel control stack.

The Nova is in development, with pre-orders open for Switzerland and the EU initially. Specifications, availability, and timelines are RY3T's to announce — [nova.ry3t.com](https://nova.ry3t.com/en/) is the place to watch.

## Why This Matters to Us

The 256 Foundation identified firmware lock-in as one of the critical gaps in Bitcoin mining infrastructure. We kicked off Mujina to close it, and we fund Core Contributors who maintain it. Through grant agreements, they dedicate their time to closing the gaps we've identified.

What we do not do is control what happens next. RY3T is taking Mujina into a market we have never touched and would not know how to serve — Swiss and EU homeowners with solar arrays and full batteries. We could not have planned that, staffed it, or sold into it. We did not have to. The license did the work.

Two things follow from the Nova existing:

**Open-source mining firmware is production-ready.** Not a demo and not a hobby build — a commercial product a company is prepared to put its name and its warranty behind.

**The stack is available to everyone.** Whatever RY3T can build on Mujina, you can build on Mujina. The code is public, the license is GPLv3, and the maintainers are reachable. Take it and go.

In April, the Bitcoin community [voted to put the MARA Foundation's inaugural community contribution behind open-source Bitcoin mining infrastructure](/newsroom/mara-foundation-tier1-supporter). This is what that looks like three months later — not a progress report from us, but a product from someone else.

That is the return on funding this work. Mining is moving off the data center floor — into homes, onto solar arrays, into buildings and businesses and places the manufacturers never designed for. Firmware written for a fixed setpoint in a warehouse cannot follow it there. Open firmware can, and now there is a product on the market that proves it.
